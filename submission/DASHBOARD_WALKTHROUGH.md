# Dashboard walkthrough after the PowerPoint

Present the eight main slides first, then use this walkthrough to demonstrate the evidence. Allow approximately 8–10 minutes for the dashboard in addition to the slide presentation. If the assessed recording has a shorter limit, use the dashboard as a separate demonstration or during questions.

Route: Data & evidence → Research: Clean & renewables → Research: Coal → World: China → World: Germany → United States → Data & evidence → final recommendation slide.

## How the dashboard supports the recommendation

The dashboard lets judges trace a slide claim to its source, units, time window and observations. Use it to verify findings, examine counterexamples and inspect downloadable evidence. Its role is to make the argument reviewable; it does not estimate stock returns or establish causality.

## Short demonstration — about two minutes

1. **Verify, 30 seconds:** open Data & evidence and show E01/E02. Say: “These are the same fixed-panel calculations used in the presentation. Both clean and fossil generation grew.”
2. **Interpret, 35 seconds:** open China’s demand/mix chart, then its coal chart. Say: “Clean expansion and coal growth coexist here. Coal is shown separately from all fossil generation.”
3. **Challenge, 35 seconds:** show national U.S. consumption. Say: “The 2025 rebound is the strongest challenge to the underweight. We will reconsider if it persists.”
4. **Inspect, 20 seconds:** return to coverage and downloads. Say: “Reviewers can inspect observations and claim references. Latest-year coverage and source vintages limit our confidence.”

Use a separate demonstration or questions if the main recording limit leaves no dashboard time. These timings are rehearsal estimates.

## Interpretation checks to explain aloud

- Read units and scope before comparing: Ember and EI generation are in TWh; EIA consumption is in short tons.
- The year selector changes map and snapshot values; historical charts retain their series. Compare the same endpoints explicitly.
- Rising clean share can coexist with rising fossil output. Aggregate share is summed clean generation divided by summed total generation, not an unweighted country mean.
- Missing 2025 observations are not zeros. The 91-country panel covers 93.5% of generation observed in the 2024 extract, not 93.5% of world generation.
- Demand, generation and net imports form an accounting comparison. They do not identify policy, weather or fuel-price causes.
- State observations identify consumption locations, not mines. National sector 98 and reported state base sectors have separate scopes.
- For each expanded chart, name the question, point to endpoints, state the result, then explain whether it supports or challenges the recommendation.

## Country and sector guidance — presentation slide 6

- **United States / domestic thermal coal:** demonstrate national EIA consumption and the 2025 rebound. The conditional underweight applies to producers exposed to domestic power demand; state consumption does not identify mines or producer revenues.
- **China / low-carbon generation owners:** open China’s clean/fossil history and separate coal chart. Clean and coal both expanded through 2024. This supports investigating clean-generation exposure without assuming immediate coal displacement; examine contracts, cash flow and financing.
- **Germany / utilities and generation owners:** show demand, clean generation, coal and electricity trade. Falling clean output alongside falling coal means the investment question needs demand and revenue-exposure analysis.

China and Germany are research priorities, not country buy ratings. The dashboard supplies operational context; valuation and company financials are still required before selecting investments.

## Before presenting

- Load the dashboard and check that the data appears.
- Rehearse the searches for China and Germany and the map selection of Indiana. Use the U.S. map +/− controls to resize, drag to reposition, and Reset map to restore the overview.
- Keep the final PowerPoint slide ready to return to.
- Use Expand on charts so the audience can read them; Close returns to the dashboard.
- The year slider changes the map and selected-year snapshot. Charts retain their full historical series. When discussing 2020–2024, point to those years even though 2025 is visible.
- Map scales follow the selected year and layer or sector. Compare numeric values, rather than color alone, across selections.
- Keep screenshots of the planned views as a backup.

## 1. Transition from the PowerPoint

Leave the final recommendation slide visible and say:

> We’ve presented our recommendation. Now we’ll use the dashboard to show the evidence behind it: clean growth, differences between countries, and the U.S. coal rebound that challenges our conclusion.

Switch to the dashboard.

## 2. Establish that the dashboard supports the presentation — 40 seconds

Click **Data & evidence** in the top navigation. Scroll to **Numbers supporting the presentation**. Point to the clean-generation, all-fossil-generation and U.S. coal-consumption rows.

Say:

> These are the same core calculations supporting our presentation. Between 2020 and 2024, clean generation grew 25.4%, while fossil generation also grew 10.3%. U.S. power-sector coal consumption declined through 2024, then rebounded in 2025.
>
> Our starting finding is that clean growth and fossil resilience can happen together.

Point briefly to the Scope column:

> Each comparison keeps its source and measurement clear. Electricity generation and coal consumption measure different things.

Return to source details at the end rather than reading all the tables now.

## 3. Show that clean generation is gaining ground — 60 seconds

Click **Research**, then **Clean & renewables**. Scroll to the chart **Matched-sample clean share of generation** and click **Expand**. Point to its beginning and end and describe the displayed trend.

Say:

> This chart shows how clean electricity’s share changes within a fixed group of countries. Clean includes renewables and nuclear.
>
> The important question is whether clean sources are gaining a larger share of electricity generation over time.

Click **Close**. Find **Matched-sample wind + solar generation** and click **Expand**.

Say:

> This second chart shows actual wind and solar output. Looking at output alongside share helps us distinguish more electricity being generated from a change in the percentage mix.
>
> These research charts use a fixed six-year sample. That sample differs from the broader historical panel used in our presentation, so we keep their totals separate.

Click **Close**. Transition:

> But growing clean generation does not automatically mean coal generation is falling.

## 4. Show why the coal conclusion needs geographic context — 45 seconds

Click **Coal** within Research. Find **World coal generation (Total World row)** and click **Expand**. Point to 2020 and 2024, then 2025 separately.

Say:

> This is the Energy Institute’s reported world total for coal-fired generation. Through 2024, coal generation increased even while clean generation was expanding.
>
> That is why our clean-energy recommendation does not depend on an immediate worldwide collapse in coal.

If discussing 2025, describe its displayed change separately from the longer historical trend. Click **Close**. Briefly point to the matched-sample chart below:

> The chart below tracks a fixed country sample. It has a different scope from the world total above.

Transition:

> The country explorer lets us see how these patterns develop within individual markets.

## 5. China: explain how clean and coal can grow together — 90 seconds

Click **World**. Set the year to **2024**. Set the Layer dropdown to **Clean electricity share**. Type **China** in the country search field and press **Enter**.

Briefly show the globe and country snapshot:

> The map gives us the geographic overview. The country charts show how demand and the generation mix changed.

Scroll within the country panel to **Demand vs clean & fossil generation** and click **Expand**. Point to the dashed demand line and the two generation lines at 2020 and 2024.

Say:

> China illustrates the central finding. Between 2020 and 2024, electricity demand rose about 29.7%, and clean generation rose about 50.9%. Fossil generation also increased.
>
> Clean output grew strongly while the electricity system was expanding. Those changes can coexist with higher fossil output.

Click **Close**. Find **Coal-fired generation** and click **Expand**.

Say:

> The previous chart showed all fossil generation. This chart isolates coal. Coal generation increased about 18.4% between 2020 and 2024.

Point to 2025:

> The latest observation then shows coal generation falling from its 2024 level. That is a change worth monitoring, rather than proof of a permanent decline.

Click **Close**. Transition:

> Germany shows a different pathway—and why a coal decline needs interpretation too.

## 6. Germany: explain why falling coal is not the whole story — 90 seconds

Replace China with **Germany** in the country search and press **Enter**. Keep the selected year at **2024**.

Find **Demand vs clean & fossil generation** and click **Expand**. Point to the 2020 and 2024 endpoints.

Say:

> In Germany, demand fell about 4.6% between 2020 and 2024. Clean generation also fell, by about 7.9%.
>
> So we should not explain Germany’s coal decline simply by saying domestic clean generation increased.

Click **Close**. Find **Coal-fired generation** and click **Expand**.

Say:

> Coal generation declined about 21.4% over that period. The decline is clear, but the other charts help us understand the surrounding changes.

Click **Close**. Find **Electricity trade exposure** and click **Expand**. Point to negative values in earlier years and positive values in 2024.

Say:

> Negative values mean net exports; positive values mean net imports. Germany moved from net exports in 2020 to net imports in 2024.
>
> Demand, domestic generation, and trade all matter when interpreting the coal decline.

Click **Close**. Transition:

> These two examples explain why we keep the coal recommendation specific to the market. Now we’ll examine our strongest underweight case: U.S. domestic thermal coal.

## 7. United States: coal versus clean nationally — 60 seconds

Click **United States**. Set the year to **2024**. Start with **Generation · TWh**, showing the two snapshot cards and the national historical chart. Clean is blue, coal is red. Switch to **Share · %** to distinguish output from generation mix.

Say:

> The same coal-versus-clean comparison works nationally for the United States. Clean comes from Ember and coal from EI, so source totals can differ. The chart lets us inspect whether clean expansion coincides with falling coal, rather than assuming it does.

Set the year to **2025** and inspect the latest coal observation. Scroll to **Check the national coal-consumption evidence · EIA** and expand it. Point to the consumption chart:

> A second measure confirms the rebound direction. EIA national power-sector consumption fell 14.4% through 2024, then rose 11.3% in 2025. Consumption in short tons and generation in TWh stay separate. The rebound lowers conviction in our thermal-coal underweight.

Collapse the EIA check after explaining it.

## 8. State markets: make the coal allocation more specific — 60 seconds

At **Priority coal-demand markets · 2025**, point to the ranked table. Texas consumes 48.8 million short tons, Missouri 26.7 million and Indiana 25.2 million. Annual changes are +4.2%, +16.7% and +21.5%, respectively. These are reported power-sector base-row sums, not national sector-98 totals.

Say:

> Within a selective coal allocation, suppliers serving Texas, Missouri and Indiana warrant closer attention: these are the largest reported power-demand markets and all three grew in 2025. Texas provides scale; Missouri and Indiana show stronger recent demand growth. Our data supports market priorities, not company-specific returns or position sizes.

Click **1. Texas** to open its consumption and history. Explain the selected-year value and the longer trajectory. A large market with declining consumption should be described differently from a growing market. Missing observations stay blank.

## 9. Optional state map and key — 30–45 seconds

Open **Explore the state coal-demand map**. Use +/− and drag to inspect states. Expand **Map key**: darker orange means more coal consumed, and gray means no reported data. Annual changes remain in the tooltip, rankings and state history. Collapse it to clear the map. The map is fixed to electric-power sectors 1+2+3; industrial, quality and price controls have been removed.

Say:

> State geography shows coal customers, not mines or supplier revenues. We have no state clean-generation dataset, so we do not invent a state coal-versus-clean comparison. The national view supplies that comparison, while the state view identifies the remaining coal-demand markets.

Click a state or use the state/territory selector to inspect its power-demand history. The national EIA consumption measure and state base-sector sums retain their separate scopes.

## 10. Finish with evidence access and the recommendation — 45–60 seconds

Click **Data & evidence**. Briefly show **Coverage by source and year**.

Say:

> Coverage differs by source and year, particularly for the latest observations. Missing data stays missing, and our comparisons use the stated country panels.

Scroll to **Country data directory**. Type **China** into **Country name or ISO code…**. Point to source-year coverage and the **Explore** and **CSV** buttons. Do not download files during the presentation unless needed for a question.

Say:

> Anyone reviewing our findings can check country coverage, reopen a country, or download its observations.

Briefly show **Download the supporting data**:

> The presentation calculations, claim references, source definitions, and recommendation are also available here.

Return to the final PowerPoint slide and close:

> The dashboard supports a selective conclusion: clean generation is growing, coal follows different paths across markets, and the U.S. rebound is a material risk to our underweight. That is why we favor clean growth while keeping the coal call focused and conditional.

Take questions.

## Extra charts to keep for questions

- **What met the change in demand?** Annual clean, fossil and net-import changes alongside demand change; an accounting comparison, not causal attribution.
- **Carbon intensity of power** and **Emissions versus emissions intensity**: distinguish emissions per kWh from total power emissions.
- **Coal versus gas generation**: compare EI fuel-generation series without treating all fossil output as coal.
- **Coal production versus coal generation**: compare indexed directions; divergence alone does not identify exports, inventories or non-power demand.
- **Compare with another country**: choose a peer with overlapping clean-share observations; a higher share does not imply greater absolute clean generation.
- **Underlying country observations**: inspect reported annual values and download the country/year evidence.
- **Research → Utilities & retail / Gas & grid / Cross-country**: supporting discussion if asked; keep the main walkthrough focused on the recommendation.
