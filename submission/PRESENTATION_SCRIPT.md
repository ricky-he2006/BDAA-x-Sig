# Presentation script

Eight main slides plus five evidence appendix slides · 782 main-pitch spoken words · planned main-pitch delivery 6:15. Appendix narration adds time and is excluded from that plan. Speaker labels A–D are suggestions; adjust for your group. Delivery cues are not spoken.

## 1. Invest in clean growth. Stay selective on coal.

**Speaker A · 0:00–0:35 · 35 seconds**

Our recommendation is to overweight clean energy and underweight coal over the next three years. We would focus the clean position on diversified low-carbon electricity generation, and the coal underweight on U.S. domestic thermal producers.

The reason is simple: clean electricity is expanding, while coal follows very different paths across markets. We’ll show how the generation mix changed, why geography matters, and how the U.S. rebound challenges our view. The recommendation comes with clear conditions for reconsideration.

*Delivery cue: State both calls and the U.S. thermal-coal scope before explaining the evidence.*

## 2. Clean grew faster. Fossil output still increased.

**Speaker A · 0:35–1:25 · 50 seconds**

Start with what the electricity system actually produced. Between 2020 and 2024, clean generation increased 25.4 percent across the same 196 countries. All-fossil generation also rose, by 10.3 percent. Clean includes renewables and nuclear.

The blue line shows faster clean growth, while the orange line shows that fossil volumes remained resilient. A cleaner mix can therefore coexist with more fossil output. We hold country coverage constant so that missing observations cannot create a false decline. Ember supplies this system view; EIA and our third dataset, the Energy Institute, let us examine coal more closely. The aggregate, however, hides different country outcomes.

*Delivery cue: Point to the two endpoints. Explain that the lines are generation indices, not investment returns.*

## 3. The coal call needs a market context.

**Speaker B · 1:25–2:10 · 45 seconds**

Three examples show why the geographic scope matters. China increased both clean and coal generation through 2024: that is coexistence. The United States combined rising clean output with falling coal generation over the same period.

Germany gives us a useful caution. Coal generation fell there, but clean output and electricity demand also fell. That result alone cannot establish clean-energy substitution. These are observed outcomes, rather than explanations of what caused them. For investors, the implication is to specify the market and business model behind a coal position. The latest year makes that distinction even more important.

*Delivery cue: Use the three examples as illustrations; do not read a list of countries.*

## 4. 2025 strengthened clean growth. Coal moved differently by market.

**Speaker B · 2:10–3:05 · 55 seconds**

In 2025, clean-generation additions were about 110 percent of additional electricity demand in our 91-country Ember panel. All-fossil output fell in that sample. This is a consistent comparison, not a full-world estimate: the panel represents 93.5 percent of generation observed in the 2024 extract.

The right panel asks a separate question using EI coal generation. China and India declined, while the United States increased. That supports the case for clean expansion, while showing that coal can move against the broader pattern in individual markets. One annual decline does not establish a lasting global peak. The U.S. increase is the strongest counterargument to our coal underweight.

*Delivery cue: Explain the left panel, then the right. Keep the two sources and fuel definitions separate.*

## 5. The coal rebound is real. Persistence is the risk.

**Speaker C · 3:05–4:00 · 55 seconds**

EIA confirms the rebound in a different measure: coal consumed by the U.S. electric-power sector. Consumption fell 14.4 percent from 2020 to 2024, then rose 11.3 percent in 2025. The orange bar makes that recovery clear.

Delivered prices were slightly lower, moving from 46 dollars and 45 cents to 45 dollars and 56 cents per short ton. Those are fuel costs, so they do not tell us producer margins. The supplied files also do not establish the cause of the rebound. Our judgment is that the historical decline still warrants caution, but the recovery lowers our conviction. If it persists, we would reconsider the underweight.

*Delivery cue: Distinguish EIA consumption in short tons from the previous slide’s generation in TWh.*

## 6. Allocate to clean growth. Focus the coal underweight.

**Speaker C · 4:00–4:55 · 55 seconds**

Those findings lead to two industry positions. First, overweight diversified low-carbon generation exposure. Clean output expanded across different market conditions, including markets where coal remained important. Diversification avoids making the whole position depend on one technology or developer.

Second, underweight coal with the clearest emphasis on U.S. domestic thermal producers. We would not automatically extend that conclusion to Asian exporters or metallurgical coal, whose exposures need separate evidence. The horizon is three years, relative to neutral energy-allocation weights. Utilities must manage fuel mix, while developers must finance profitable projects. Before selecting securities, we still need valuations, project returns and financing data. Generation growth alone cannot establish attractive investment returns.

*Delivery cue: Name the exposure, explain the logic, then distinguish industry direction from security selection.*

## 7. What would change our view?

**Speaker D · 4:55–5:40 · 45 seconds**

We would monitor three things. For coal, reconsider the U.S. underweight if consumption remains above its 2024 level for two further annual releases. For clean energy, reassess the overweight after two comparable years without clean-share gains, alongside rising fossil generation.

For the investments themselves, weak project returns or financing pressure would require another look at security selection. These are analyst decision rules, not fitted forecasts. We also retain the source limitations: incomplete country coverage, differences between data vintages, and an unverified original edition and download date for the local EI file. Those limits constrain the confidence and precision of the recommendation.

*Delivery cue: Explain the monitoring rules as judgment calls, not forecasts or estimated probabilities.*

## 8. Own clean growth. Keep coal exposure selective.

**Speaker D · 5:40–6:15 · 35 seconds**

Our conclusion is to overweight clean energy and underweight coal over three years, with the coal position focused on U.S. domestic thermal exposure. The evidence supports a growing clean-electricity industry and an uneven outlook for coal.

We would track generation additions, U.S. coal consumption and the economics of the investments available. The position should change when the evidence changes. For now, our recommendation is to participate in clean-power expansion and keep the coal underweight focused.

*Delivery cue: Finish with the allocation and horizon. Pause after the final sentence.*

## 10. Preserve missingness. Prevent false comparisons.

**Appendix A02 · additional narration · suggested speaker A**

Ember coverage shrinks from 213 geographies in 2020 to 196 in 2024 and 91 in 2025. A naive annual sum could confuse missing countries with decline. Fixed membership prevents that error but does not remove selection bias. Missing prices cannot be imputed as zero. The pipeline retains flags and source values; its checks cover grain uniqueness, missing versus zero, sector separation and accounting. Show the actual audit outputs if asked; do not claim every reported cross-source number matches.

## 11. Faster clean growth survives a different baseline

**Appendix A03 · additional narration · suggested speaker B**

We use descriptive accounting because the question asks whether generation expanded together, not to estimate a causal policy effect. Aggregate share is summed clean TWh divided by summed total TWh, not an unweighted country mean. Clean additions divided by demand additions is only meaningful with positive demand growth; it does not prove causal substitution. No confidence interval is fitted to these supplied snapshot totals. Uncertainty comes from revisions, definitions, coverage and the forward-looking allocation judgment.
